First Principles

“What is absolutely true, and what is just an assumption?”

Used by highly successful individuals like Elon Musk & Ray Dalio, First Principles thinking is the art of deconstructing complex questions and identifying the fundamental truths beneath them. The goal is to cut through assumptions and isolate the principal causal relationships underlying a problem.

Core Subcomponents

Reframe The Question Into A Goal

Transform the original question into a clear, outcome-oriented objective. Instead of accepting the problem at face value, redefine it in terms of what you ultimately want to achieve. This forces clarity, eliminates ambiguity, and aligns your thinking with measurable results rather than vague assumptions.

Challenge The Validity of Popular Assumptions

Identify the assumptions embedded in the problem — especially the ones people rarely question. Examine which of them are based on evidence, which are outdated, and which are simply inherited from conventional thinking. By stripping away weak or untested assumptions, you expose the real structure of the problem.

How to Challenge Assumptions
  • List every assumption.
  • Ask “Is this proven?”
  • Remove unproven assumptions.
  • Rebuild using only facts.
Use Common Sense

Once the question is reframed and assumptions are removed, evaluate the remaining elements through simple, rational judgment. Common sense helps avoid over-engineering solutions and keeps the analysis grounded in reality. It’s a check against unnecessary complexity and ensures the final solution is both practical and effective.

Using Common Sense
  • Choose the simplest solution.
  • Check if it’s realistic.
  • Cut unnecessary steps.
  • Act rationally.
By Whom Is It Used?

Hedge fund managers, elite negotiators, senior engineers, and theoretical physicists rely on First Principles thinking because their fields demand solutions that go beyond conventional logic. In high-stakes environments—such as portfolio construction, crisis negotiation, or designing spacecraft—assumptions can distort decision-making. These professionals strip problems down to their fundamental variables so they can model behavior, predict outcomes, and construct strategies that outperform traditional, assumption-based thinking.

Mental Model Stacking

“What’s another way to look at the exact same problem?”

Mental model stacking is the practice of applying multiple frameworks or lenses from different disciplines to gain a deeper, more accurate understanding of a problem. Instead of relying on one approach, you layer several mental models to test, challenge, and enhance your solution from different angles.

By Whom Is It Used?

Used by multidisciplinary thinkers such as management consultants, investors, systems engineers, and high-level strategists who need to integrate insights across psychology, economics, engineering, biology, statistics, and philosophy. These professionals avoid 'single-lens thinking' and instead rely on a toolkit of frameworks to process complexity.

Core Subcomponents

Core Mechanics

The key underpinnings of Mental Model Stacking.

Core Mechanics
  • Cross-Domain Thinking: Pull models from psychology, economics, physics, game theory, systems thinking, etc.
  • Contrast & Compare: Use models that intentionally conflict or complement each other.
  • Stress-Test Ideas: Ask: “If this model explains part of the problem, what might it be missing?”
  • Synthesize: Combine the best elements of each lens into a more resilient solution.

The OODA Loop

“What’s the major decision workflow I need to follow under limited time constraints?”

The OODA Loop — short for Observe, Orient, Decide, Act — is a rapid decision-making framework originally developed by U.S. Air Force Colonel John Boyd for fighter pilots. It’s now widely used in business, strategy, and high-stakes environments where speed, clarity, and adaptability win. It’s not a one-time loop — it’s a continuous, self-correcting feedback system that helps you adapt your decisions as new information becomes available.

By Whom Is It Used?

Used by fighter pilots, military commanders, security analysts, hedge fund traders, emergency responders, and business leaders operating in fast-changing, high-stakes environments where speed and adaptability determine success.

Core Subcomponents

Observe

Gather raw data, signals, anomalies, and inputs. Detect real-time changes in environment or system conditions.

Orient

Apply filters: experience, culture, mental models, prior knowledge. Challenge assumptions, biases, and outdated perspectives.

Decide

Frame decision criteria and define success metrics. Choose the most viable path under current information.

Act

Carry out the decision at speed and watch for new feedback. Reinforce agility by re-entering the loop as needed.

Schumpeter's Theory of Creative Destruction

“What needs to be broken and rebuilt for progress to happen?”

Joseph Schumpeter’s theory views innovation as creative destruction – the process of dismantling old systems, processes, or products to make room for new, better ones. In business and strategy, this means that true innovation doesn’t just add on – it displaces.

By Whom Is It Used?

Economists, innovators, product designers, venture capitalists, and disruptive entrepreneurs use Creative Destruction to understand and exploit the cycles of innovation that reshape industries. Companies like Tesla, Apple, NVIDIA, and Amazon actively apply this model to outpace incumbents.

Core Subcomponents

Map The Status Quo

What systems, roles, technologies, or workflows are in place?

Challenge the Premise

Why do these exist? What assumptions are they built on?

Spot the Bottlenecks

Which ones limit speed quality, or scale?

Create the Replacement

“Design the solution that renders the old one irrelevant.”

Bayesian Inferencing

“What’s the probability of these events happening?”

Bayesian Inferencing is a statistical technique that involves updating the probability of your assumptions when new information enters the environment. The core focus is probabilistic thinking, recalibrating your assumptions as you obtain more information.

By Whom Is It Used?

Data scientists, AI researchers, quantitative analysts, poker players, epidemiologists, and intelligence agencies use Bayesian reasoning to update beliefs under uncertainty and make probability-driven decisions.

Major Components

You start with a prior belief (your initial assumption). When new evidence comes in, you revise that belief into a posterior probability using logic, not gut feeling.It answers: “Now that I know this, how likely is that?

Variables & Formula
  • A Priori (P(B)): “What was your initial belief?”
  • A Posteriori (P(A)): “What’s the base probability of your initial belief?”
  • Likelihood of Information Given Hypothesis Is True (P(A|B)): “What’s the likelihood that your initial belief is true?”

Neyman-Pearson Hypothesis Testing Framework

“Is the change real?”

The Neyman-Pearson framework formalizes decision-making under uncertainty — where you test competing hypotheses and minimize the risk of being wrong. It’s not about "maybe"; it's about rigorously rejecting bad ideas and defending good ones with statistical strength.

By Whom Is It Used?

Statisticians, analysts, product teams, financial researchers, pharmaceutical companies, and economists use hypothesis testing to validate decisions with statistical rigor rather than intuition or anecdote.

🧠 Core Concepts:

Key variables that drive the hypothesis-testing framework.

Variables & Inputs
  • Null Hypothesis (H₀): Default assumption (e.g., “This strategy has no effect.”)
  • Alternative Hypothesis (H₁): Your proposed solution or insight (e.g., “This strategy works.”)
  • Significance Level (α): How much error you're willing to accept (usually 0.05 or 5%)
  • Power (1 - β): Probability of correctly detecting a real effect (i.e., not missing the truth)
Methodology

Process of testing and rejecting null hypotheses to achieve a true statistical effect.

🔄 The Process:
Step Description
Frame the Problem Rigorously
  • H₀: “The marketing campaign has no effect on conversion.”
  • H₁: “The campaign significantly improves conversion.”
Collect Data
  • Run A/B tests, observe behaviors, simulate outcomes.
Set a Narrow Significance Threshold
  • α = 0.01 means only a 1% chance you'd accept a false positive.
Calculate the Test Statistic
  • T-stat, Z-stat, or custom metric — based on sample size, variance, and effect size.
Compare Against Critical Value
  • If your result crosses the threshold, reject H₀.
  • If not, you don’t “prove” H₀ — you just don’t have enough to reject it.

Adversarial Thinking

“How will my adversaries respond?”

Adversarial thinking is the mindset of building systems, strategies, and arguments that withstand intelligent attack. You don’t focus on how to beat your opponent — you focus on ensuring they can’t beat you. This means preemptively identifying weaknesses, anticipating counterplay, and pressure-testing your position from the perspective of a motivated adversary.

By Whom Is It Used?

Cybersecurity engineers, military planners, esports professionals, intelligence analysts, negotiation experts, and management consultants use adversarial thinking to build systems resilient to intelligent attackers.

Core Applications

Where it is used.

🔍 Why It Matters:
  • Project Management You don’t just create timelines — you design them to survive scope creep, stakeholder volatility, and unforeseen bottlenecks.
  • Finance: You don’t just build a model — you stress test it under black swan scenarios.
  • Negotiation: You don’t just state your ask — you anticipate their no, and prepare for their BATNA.
  • Entrepreneurship: You don’t just pitch your product — you address what competitors will say behind closed doors.
Core Components

Rundown of core components that comprise Adversarial Thinking in practice.

Core Components
  • Red Team Your Strategies: Simulate the perspective of a prospective adversary.
  • Pre-Mortem Analysis: Consider future possible failures and ask why they would happen.
  • Play the Devil's Advocate: Argue against popular, contrary beliefs with rational judgment.
  • Game Theory Application: Assume your opponent acts rationally in their best interest - not yours.
  • Avoid Crushing Your Opponent: Adversarial Thinking is not about crushing your opponent (unlike the 48 Laws of Power may say). It's about ensuring your opponent doesn't crush you.
Vulnerabilities:

What to look out for and avoid.

Vulnerabilities
Concept Description
Streisand Effect

“If you try to suppress the wrong voice, you may give them a microphone.”

Definition: Efforts to suppress or censor criticism can amplify it to mass attention. Attempting to silence an adversary may validate their claims and drive public interest.
Blind Spot Exploitation

“What you refuse to see is exactly what will be used against you.”

Definition: Fixed mindsets or unchallenged assumptions make you easy to outmaneuver by creative or unconventional opponents.
Dependency Trap

“If your power rests on one pipe, your enemy only needs one wrench.”

Definition: Overreliance on one vendor, channel, ally, or system becomes a strategic chokepoint.
Information Asymmetry

“He who frames the question, frames the battlefield.”

Definition: Adversaries who know more than you will shape the terrain of engagement to their advantage.
Fragile Identity Attachments

“The fastest way to collapse an empire is to insult its ruler’s ego.”

Definition: When decisions are made to protect pride, legacy, or narrative control rather than sound reasoning.

Cognitive Load Management

“How do I manage the weight?”

This model is about managing the limited bandwidth of your attention, decision-making power, and focus—especially under complexity, fatigue, and pressure. It isn’t just about avoiding burnout; it’s about deploying your cognitive horsepower with precision.

By Whom Is It Used?

Executives, students, surgeons, pilots, programmers, athletes, and anyone under high mental workload use cognitive load strategies to protect decision-making clarity and maintain peak performance.

Agile vs. Waterfall Goal Setting:

Waterfall goal setting involves planning a large list, and completing goals one at a time rather than iteratively setting goals at the source as you progress along.

Agile vs. Waterfall
  • Waterfall: Plan all goals ahead of time, execute in sequence. Good for known, rigid timelines (e.g., course schedules, CFA modules).
  • Agile: Adapt goals continuously as new inputs emerge. Best for open-ended projects, startups, or unclear scope.
    • Use Agile when navigating ambiguity.
    • Use Waterfall when executional stability matters more than flexibility.
Kanban vs. Scrum

Kanban is a project management methodology emphasizing cutting work in progress, emphasizing continuous flow state optimization. Scrum, on the other hand, focuses on working in Sprints to deliver content efficiently and on point. Both are important for managing load - you will see how I use either.

Kanban vs Scrum
Kanban - Flow State Optimization:
  • Break goals into visible cards (To Do / Doing / Done).
  • Limit Work-In-Progress (WIP) to prevent overload.
  • Triggers dopamine with visual progress → better momentum and mental clarity.
Scrum - Working In Sprints
  • Fixed time blocks (e.g. 7-day sprints)
  • Each sprint focuses on a few high-impact goals, not everything.
  • Sprint retrospectives allow you to learn and adapt rapidly without scope bloat.
Goal Slicing

The process of dividing rather difficult goals into subgoals - managing load & fueling flow state through visible progress.

Goal Slicing
  • Divide complex, vague tasks into "atomic" actionable steps.
  • Reduces resistance to starting. Easier to stack wins.
  • Example: "Read CFA Ethics" -> "Open CFA module" -> "Read 1st Vignette" -> "Take 3 Practice Questions".

Behavioral Biases

“What stops my better judgment?”

Used by highly successful individuals like Elon Musk & Ray Dalio, First Principles thinking is the art of deconstructing complex questions and identifying the fundamental truths beneath them. The goal is to cut through assumptions and isolate the principal causal relationships underlying a problem.

By Whom Is It Used?

Investors, psychologists, policymakers, marketers, UX designers, and risk managers use behavioral bias frameworks to predict and correct irrational human behavior.

Rational Biases

Even with the best frameworks, humans are not always rational. These biases distort how we perceive risks, make decisions, and interpret outcomes — especially under pressure, fatigue, or ambiguity.

Rational Biases
Category Description

Expect mental shortcuts that feel efficient, but lack depth.
Signs
  • You're locked onto a first guess, forecast, or number.
  • You believe your judgment is more accurate than it actually is.
  • You feel “logically confident” without validating your premise.
Counteractions
  • Ask: What would disprove this? Don’t just seek validation — actively look for refutation. Pretend you're a skeptic and test your thesis like a prosecutor.
  • Benchmark against real-world data. Intuition can lie. External comparisons (market stats, precedent cases, actual probabilities) keep your assumptions grounded.
  • Recalculate with fresh numbers. If you anchored to a first number or forecast, strip it away. Redo the estimate from scratch using different data.
  • Pause and delay intuitive decisions. If it felt “obvious” in under 30 seconds, flag it. Slow down — your brain may be skipping steps.
Emotional Biases

These come fast. A wave of fear, pride, attachment, or regret hits — and it subtly pulls your decision off-course. You might not even notice the emotion; you’ll just justify your choice afterward with surface logic.

Rational Biases
Category Description

Expect gut reactions that override logic.
Signs
  • You're more focused on avoiding pain than making gains.
  • You can’t let go of something you built, even if it's failing.
  • You feel uneasy about a decision — not because it's wrong, but because it might lead to regret.
Counteractions
  • Name the emotion. Ask yourself: What am I afraid of losing? What emotion is influencing this choice? Labeling the emotion defuses its power.
  • Reverse the lens. If a friend were in your exact position, what would you tell them to do? This helps detach identity from the decision.
  • Time-shift your perspective. Think: How would I feel about this decision one year from now? Short-term emotions lose their grip when stretched across time.
  • Accept regret as a cost of doing business. Not all regret is avoidable. Decisions aren’t about minimizing regret — they’re about maximizing wisdom.
Social Biases:

You’ll feel smarter, safer, or more correct when your idea matches the group’s — even if you know it’s not the best call. These biases are especially sneaky because they often feel like common sense or social intuition.

Rational Biases
Category Description

Expect your thoughts to subtly shift in group settings or around “authority.”
Signs
  • You're locked onto a first guess, forecast, or number.
  • You believe your judgment is more accurate than it actually is.
  • You feel “logically confident” without validating your premise.
Counteractions
  • Do a “silent brainstorm” before group work. Write your thoughts independently before any discussion begins. This preserves your raw, unfiltered thinking.
  • Deliberately ask the “stupid question.” One honest dissent often opens the door for others. Be the person who challenges the room — respectfully.
  • Evaluate ideas, not speakers. Strip away names and titles. If the same argument came from someone junior, would you still agree with it?
  • Use “red team” exercises. Role-play opposition. Assign someone (or yourself) to argue the other side with full seriousness. This forces deeper scrutiny.

Antifragility

“How do I gain from pressure?”

Antifragility isn’t just resilience. It’s growth under pressure. It’s the mindset, systems, and mechanisms that feed on chaos, volatility, or failure to become stronger — not weaker. While fragile systems break and resilient systems endure, antifragile systems adapt, improve, and evolve.

By Whom Is It Used?

Traders, entrepreneurs, military strategists, systems architects, evolutionary biologists, and high-performance athletes rely on antifragility to gain from stressors rather than merely survive them.

Core Concepts

The key concepts underpinning antifragility.

Core Concepts
Concept Description
Optionality You structure your work/life to have more upside than downside in volatile situations. Like a call option, you limit downside and let positive shocks scale.
Asymmetric Exposure Take small, reversible risks often. Avoid large, irreversible bets. The system benefits from small failures because they’re survivable and instructive.
Stress-Informed Feedback Loops You actively monitor failure points — not to avoid stress, but to harness it early before it compounds.
Antifragile Tactics In High-Stress Environments:

The key concepts underpinning antifragility.

Core Concepts
  • Debrief After Every Crash: Write down what cracked. Then write what didn’t. Build on what survived.
  • Design for chaos: Build schedules, habits, and systems that get tighter under load — not looser.
  • • Use “pre-mortems": Assume failure before it happens. Simulate stress tests and build defenses ahead of time.
  • Turn reputational loss into insight gain: Frame mistakes as public experiments. Learn fast. Teach others faster.
  • Stay overprepared in what you can control: Sleep. Fuel. Notes. Processes. They're boring until they’re the reason you survive.
